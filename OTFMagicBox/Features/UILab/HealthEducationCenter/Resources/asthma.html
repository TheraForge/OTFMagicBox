Living Well with Asthma

Asthma is a long-term lung condition that can make the airways narrow and inflamed. Symptoms may come and go, and each person can have different triggers and warning signs. With an appropriate plan, many people can reduce symptoms and continue their usual activities.

General education only. This article cannot diagnose asthma or replace a personal treatment plan. Contact a qualified health professional about breathing symptoms. Seek emergency help for severe breathing difficulty or when your action plan tells you to do so.

Common symptoms

Asthma may cause wheezing, coughing, shortness of breath, or chest tightness. Symptoms can be worse at night, early in the morning, during activity, or after contact with a trigger. Other conditions can cause similar symptoms, so professional assessment matters.

Triggers are personal

Possible triggers include smoke, air pollution, respiratory infections, pollen, dust mites, mold, animals, strong odors, cold air, exercise, and workplace exposures. Not every trigger affects every person. Tracking where and when symptoms occur can help a care team identify useful patterns.

Support good control

  • Use controller and reliever medicines exactly as prescribed.
  • Keep an up-to-date written asthma action plan and know its symptom zones.
  • Learn correct inhaler technique and ask for it to be checked at appointments.
  • Reduce known triggers where practical without avoiding activity unnecessarily.
  • Review worsening symptoms or more frequent reliever use with a clinician.

Prepare before symptoms worsen

Keep prescribed medicine accessible, share the action plan with people who may need it, and know how to contact routine and urgent care. Ask your clinician how respiratory infections, travel, exercise, work, or seasonal changes should affect your plan.

When urgent help is needed

Follow the emergency steps in your asthma action plan. Contact emergency services when breathing is severely difficult, symptoms worsen quickly, speaking or usual movement becomes difficult, or prescribed reliever medicine is not helping as directed.
